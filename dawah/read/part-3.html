The Islamic method for atheists

🔊 Listen to this section · শুনুন

The dawah organisations teach four things before any single argument.

1. Fitrah first. Belief in God is natural, not learned. Yaqeen Institute's case for God (Justin Parrott) rests on signs that reach “both the heart (via intuition) and the mind (through rational thought)”: Quran 30:30 (the natural disposition on which God created mankind), 10:22-23 and 29:65 (people call on God alone when the ship is sinking), and the hadith “every child is born upon the fitrah” (Bukhari and Muslim). Nazir Khan's paper on Ibn Taymiyyah adds that if you doubt the God-sense you have no principled place to stop before doubting reason and the senses too. In China the fitrah shows in the language: 老天爷, 谢天谢地, 天啊.বাংলা 老天爷 (lǎotiānyé, “Old Man Heaven”) = উপরওয়ালা; 谢天谢地 (xiè tiān xiè dì, “thank heaven and earth”) = যাক, উপরওয়ালার দয়ায় বাঁচা গেল; 天啊 (tiān a, “Oh heavens!”) = হায় আসমান! (আমাদের “হায় আল্লাহ!”-এর মতো)। অর্থাৎ চীনা মানুষের মুখের ভাষাতেও স্রষ্টার দিকে স্বাভাবিক টান রয়ে গেছে।

2. The Quran's own argument, 52:35-36. “Were they created by nothing, or were they themselves the creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth?” Hamza Tzortzis reads this as a complete set of options for anything that began: from nothing (absurd: nothing has no power), self-created (contradiction: you must exist before you act), created by something itself created (leads back to the same question), or created by something uncreated. Only the last stands. Bukhari records that Jubayr ibn Mut'im, still a pagan, heard these verses and “his heart almost began to soar”.

3. The dependency argument (Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali, al-Razi; Tzortzis's “Divine Link”). Everything we see is dependent: it could have not existed and relies on something else. A chain of dependent things, even an infinite one, cannot explain itself, as an infinite row of falling dominoes does not explain why any domino fell. So there must be an independent, necessary, eternal being. This is not a God-of-the-gaps move: it reasons from what we know (dependence), not from what we do not know. Mohammed Hijab's The Burhan (Sapience Institute) develops the same proof.

4. The conversation, not the debate. Yaqeen's apologetics guidelines (Hatem al-Haj): building certainty is “always superior to answering doubts”; “intellectual debates rarely change deeply-held beliefs directly, often masking deeper emotional discontent or social bonds.” iERA's training template, GORAP (God's existence, Oneness, Revelation, Prophethood), opens by asking what the person believes. iERA later dropped its science-miracles line; the organisations now put the philosophical and fitrah arguments first.

Sources: Yaqeen: The case for Allah's existence; Yaqeen: Atheism and radical skepticism (Ibn Taymiyyah); Tzortzis: The Quran's argument for God's existence; Tzortzis: Divine Link, the argument from dependency; Sapience: The Burhan; Yaqeen: In defense of faith, guidelines for apologetics.

Theme A: Does God exist?

A1. “Who created God?” A category error: “who made X?” applies only to things that began or depend on something. The dependency argument ends in a being that is by definition uncreated; asking who created it is like asking the bachelor's wife's name. If God had a creator, that creator would be God. Verses: 52:35-36; 112:1-4; 57:3. Sources: SeekersGuidance; Islam Q&A 87677.

A2. “Why not an infinite chain of causes?” An infinite chain of dependent things is still a collection of dependent things; it cannot explain itself (the dominoes never fall without something that is not a domino). No actual infinite has ever been shown in nature, only in mathematics. Verse: 52:35-36. Source: Tzortzis, Divine Link.

A3. “The universe came from nothing (quantum vacuum).” Lawrence Krauss's “nothing” is a quantum field with laws and energy, which is something. Absolute nothing has no properties and no power. Physicists' own critiques agree quantum-gravity models do not give “universes from nothing”. Verse: 52:35. Source: Tzortzis on Krauss.

A4. “A multiverse explains fine-tuning.” A universe-generator must itself be finely tuned, so the problem moves up one level. There is no empirical evidence for other universes; a mind is a simpler explanation for specified order than infinitely many unobservable worlds. Verses: 67:3; 2:164. Source: islamreligion.com, Fine-tuning part 7.

A5. “God of the gaps.” The dependency argument reasons from what science confirms (everything observed is contingent and began), not from ignorance. Al-Ghazali and al-Razi used it centuries before modern science, so it is not a retreat. Source: Tzortzis, Divine Link (above).

A6. “The burden of proof is on you; atheism is just lack of belief.” Atheism is a claim about reality (no God) and carries a burden too; the idea that non-belief is the neutral default came from verificationism, which is itself unverifiable. Also, God-belief is the natural default (fitrah), so the one denying it must argue. Source: The Divine Reality, chapter 1; Yaqeen, Ibn Taymiyyah's critique.

A7. “Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence.” A dependent universe that is uncaused, or arose from nothing, is at least as extraordinary. The evidence for God is cumulative and ordinary (contingency, fine-tuning, consciousness, morality, the Quran). Hume's rule applied consistently would dissolve history. Source: Yaqeen, physical miracles of the Prophet.

A8. “Faith is blind.” The Quran forbids following what you have no knowledge of (17:36) and condemns blind imitation of ancestors (2:170); it commands reflection more than fifty times. Islamic faith is conviction produced by signs reaching heart and mind. Verses: 17:36; 2:170; 41:53; 2:164. Source: Bilal Philips, The True Religion (islamreligion.com).

A9. “Religion is an evolutionary by-product (hyperactive agency detection).” Explaining how a belief arose does not show it false (genetic fallacy). If our faculties are only survival-tuned, naturalism undermines trust in the reasoning used to reject God. A universal, early-emerging disposition to detect a Creator is exactly what Islam predicts; calling it a “false positive” assumes atheism. Verse: 30:30. Source: Tzortzis, Life without God.

A10. “Religion is a crutch, the opium of the people.” Marx's line is a sociological observation, not an argument about truth; comfort is irrelevant to truth, and the charge reverses (atheism relieves accountability). Islam's demands (fasting, five prayers, charity, restraint) are not a comfortable crutch. Verse: 13:28. Source: Marx's religious dilemma: an Islamic reply (Scirp).

A11. “You only believe because of where you were born.” Genetic fallacy, and it cuts both ways: an atheist born in Riyadh would likely be Muslim, and a Chinese atheist believes what Chinese schools taught. Geography explains exposure, not truth; the answer is to test the religions by reason, which Islam invites. Islam also expects diversity, since every nation received messengers. Verses: 35:24; 16:36; 2:170.

A12. “Morality does not need God.” Atheists can be moral; the claim is that atheism cannot ground morality as objectively binding. If even one moral truth is objective (torturing babies for fun is wrong), it needs a transcendent ground. And when the atheist calls suffering “evil” he borrows an objective standard his view cannot supply. Verses: 30:30; 91:7-8. Sources: Tzortzis, Know God Know Good; Yaqeen, Being a good person is not enough.

A13. Euthyphro: “Is it good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?” False dilemma. Goodness is God's essential nature; His commands flow from that nature, so good is neither external to God nor arbitrary. His names (the Merciful, the Just, the Wise) express this. Verse: 7:180. Source: Tzortzis, Divine commands.

A14. “Consciousness is just the brain.” No physical description captures subjective experience (the hard problem; even materialist philosophers admit the explanatory gap). Islam treats the soul as distinct and the brain as its instrument; the Quran limits our knowledge of the soul (17:85) and points to signs within ourselves (41:53). Source: Solving the mind-body problem with Islamic philosophy.

A15. Near-death experiences. Not an Islamic argument. The Quran describes the intermediate state as hidden from the living (23:100); mainstream Muslim writers do not use NDEs as proof. Islam's evidence for the afterlife is God's justice and the Quran. Do not lean on NDE stories in China.

Theme B: Evil, suffering, hiddenness, prayer, free will, hell

B1. The problem of evil. Yaqeen (Elshinawy; Hani): evil is “neither a logical nor a probabilistic objection to God's existence”. The logical version needs a hidden premise (God could have no good reason to permit evil) that cannot be proven; the evidential version assumes a limited human can judge what an all-wise being would permit. Islam's answer has layers: free will makes moral evil possible; life is a test (67:2, 2:155); suffering purifies, expiates sins and is compensated eternally; God's wisdom is not fully knowable (Khidr, 18:60-82); and the objection presupposes objective evil, which atheism cannot ground. Hadith: “no fatigue, disease or sorrow befalls a Muslim but God expiates sins by it” (Bukhari). Sources: Why do people suffer?; A multifaceted Islamic solution; Tzortzis, Is God merciful?.

B2. Natural disasters. Islam accepts the geology and adds meaning: reminders of mortality, tests for the afflicted and for the safe (tested in compassion), sometimes consequences of human corruption (42:30, 30:41). No one may declare a specific event a punishment; victims are compensated; children are not punished. Source: Islam Q&A 2593.

B3. Animal suffering. Animals are communities like us (6:38) and will be gathered and have wrongs settled (hadith of the hornless sheep, Muslim). They are not morally accountable, so their suffering is not punishment; Islam's welfare rules (the woman punished for a cat, the man forgiven for a dog) show God's concern. Predation is part of a wise order whose full reasons we do not know.

B4. “If God existed He would show Himself.” Overwhelming manifestation would compel belief and end the test; God communicates by inspiration, from behind a veil, or through a messenger (42:51), and could have made everyone believe (10:99) but chose a world of free choice. He is not hidden: creation, fitrah and revelation are His signs (41:53). Vision cannot grasp Him (6:103) because of transcendence, not absence. Source: Divine hiddenness, an Islamic response.

B5. “Why a book? Why not direct evidence?” A preserved, public, checkable book that makes testable claims (2:23; 4:82) is better evidence than a private vision the next person can dismiss. The Quran is the miracle every generation can examine. Source: Philips, The Challenge of the Quran.

B6. “Miracles violate natural law (Hume).” Laws describe regularities, not necessities; the Lawgiver can act outside His habit. Hume's method, applied consistently, would dissolve history. The question becomes “is this miracle well attested?”, not “are miracles possible?”. Source: Yaqeen, physical miracles.

B7. “Prayer does not work.” Every supplication free of sin is answered in one of three ways: granted now, stored as reward, or an equal harm averted (Ahmad). One is answered “so long as he does not become impatient” (Bukhari, Muslim). Prayer is worship, not a vending machine; it tests the servant, not God. Verses: 2:186; 40:60. Source: Islam Q&A 103099.

B8. Free will versus predestination. Qadar is God's eternal knowledge of and power over everything, including what people freely choose; knowing is not forcing. Islam rejected both extremes historically (denying decree, denying choice). Humans deliberate and are accountable for intention and effort; outcomes belong to God. Verses: 76:3; 18:29; 54:49. Source: Yaqeen, predestination vs free will.

B9. Hell: “infinite punishment for finite sins.” Mercy is the dominant reality (“My mercy prevails over My wrath”, Bukhari). Hell is for a settled disposition of knowing rejection, not a finite slip; there is no original sin; punishment is proportionate (4:40); many are released after purification; and a minority scholarly view (Ibn Taymiyyah, Ibn al-Qayyim) holds hell eventually ends. Verses: 4:40; 6:12; 11:106-107. Source: Yaqeen, the Infinitely Merciful and hellfire.

B10. “What about people who never heard of Islam?” “We never punish until We have sent a messenger” (17:15). Those the message never reached clearly (ahl al-fatrah) are excused and, per hadith, tested on the Day of Judgement; Muslims may not judge any individual to be in hell. Reaching monotheism by reflection is enough. This matters in China, where most people have never heard Islam explained properly. Verses: 17:15; 6:19; 4:48. Source: Yaqeen, the fate of non-Muslims.

সারকথা: মূল জবাব: দুনিয়া পরীক্ষার জায়গা; কষ্ট গুনাহ মোচন করে আর আখিরাতে পূর্ণ প্রতিদান মেলে। দোয়া কোনো না কোনোভাবে কবুল হয়, তাকদির মানে জোর করা নয়, আল্লাহর রহমত গজবের উপর প্রবল, আর যার কাছে বার্তা পৌঁছায়নি তাকে শাস্তি দেওয়া হয় না।

Theme C and D: God's nature; science and religion

C1. “Why does God need worship? Is He egotistical, a dictator?” The verse after “I created jinn and men only to worship Me” (51:56) denies that God needs anything from us (51:57). Worship benefits the worshipper: it is gratitude to the One with all perfect attributes, and it frees the heart from worshipping lesser things (money, status, ego). A dictator extracts obedience for his own benefit; God commands what benefits us and gives free will to refuse (2:256). The “needy God” framing projects human psychology onto God. Verses: 51:56-57; 35:15; 14:8. Source: Yaqeen, Why does God ask for worship?.

C2. “God's anger and jealousy are petty human emotions.” Muslims affirm God's attributes “in a manner that befits His majesty” without likening them to creatures (42:11). His anger is just displeasure at wrongdoing, which a perfectly good being must have; a God indifferent to oppression would be worse. “Jealousy” (ghayrah) in hadith means protectiveness over His rights and believers' honour, not insecurity. Verses: 42:11; 7:180. Source: Islamweb fatwa 38453.

D1. “Religion and science conflict.” The “conflict thesis” is a 19th-century story; Islamic civilisation founded observatories, hospitals and the scientific method (Ibn al-Haytham), and the Quran commands observation (2:164, 3:190-191, 96:1). Science answers how; revelation answers why and who. The dawah organisations now caution against over-claiming “scientific miracles”. Source: Yaqeen, Orientalists' fables about Islamic science.

D2. “Evolution disproves Adam.” Yaqeen (Nazir Khan, Yasir Qadhi, David Jalajel): nothing in scripture denies mutation, natural selection, hominid species or common ancestry for other life; Adam's miraculous creation is a claim about one lineage, which empirical science (which studies regularities) cannot rule out. Evolutionary naturalism (unguided processes explain everything, including mind and morality) is a philosophical add-on that fails. A Muslim may accept the science and suspend judgement on how Adam fits. Verses: 3:59; 15:28-29; 71:14, 71:17. Sources: Human Origins I; Human Origins II.

D3. “The Quran says the earth is flat; seven heavens is primitive cosmology.” “Spread out” (15:19, 71:19) describes the surface from the human viewpoint; a carpet can be spread on a curved surface, and the Quran never says “flat”. Honest apologists reject Zakir Naik's “egg-shaped” reading of 79:30 as linguistically wrong. “Seven heavens” is idiom for completeness, not an astronomical model. The Quran uses the language of its audience and makes no scientific claim here; do not over-claim. Verses: 79:30; 65:12; 39:5. Source: Ghamidi on seven heavens.

Theme E: The Quran and the Prophet

E1. “Is the Quran reliably preserved?” Preserved by parallel mass memorisation and writing in the Prophet's lifetime; Abu Bakr's compilation checked each verse against written copies, memorisers and witnesses; Uthman's codex standardised the skeletal text; the minor regional readings (qira'at) strengthen rather than weaken the transmission. See the manuscripts table in the Evidence Library. Verses: 15:9; 75:17. Sources: Yaqeen, how the Quran was preserved; The Uthmanic codex.

E2. “The Quran contradicts itself.” The Quran invites the test (4:82). Apparent contradictions dissolve when one verse is general and another specific, or when Arabic usage is known (six days versus the “eight” of 41:9-12, which sum overlapping periods; who takes the soul, God through His angels). Ask the sceptic for the specific verse; most have never read one.

E3. “Abrogation means God changed His mind.” Naskh replaces one ruling with a later one for a community in transition, not a truth with a falsehood; most cases (alcohol) are gradual restriction leading to prohibition. A doctor adjusting treatment is wisdom, not fickleness. Verses: 2:106; 16:101. Source: SeekersGuidance on naskh.

E4. “Why Arabic? A universal God would speak to everyone.” Every messenger was sent in his people's language (14:4) so the first audience could understand and be accountable; any text must have one language. Arabic was then preserved because the Quran fixed it, allowing an unbroken 1,400-year reading. Translations carry the meaning; Islam does not require Arabic to accept the message, and Ma Jian's Chinese Quran exists for this reason. Verses: 14:4; 42:7; 41:44. Source: Why was the Quran revealed in Arabic?.

E5. “The Quran was copied from the Bible.” The Quran retells shared stories to correct them (Adam, Jesus) and presents itself as confirming and guarding earlier revelation from the same God (5:48), so shared content is expected on its own thesis. Borrowing would require a literate, multilingual Prophet with Syriac and Hebrew texts in Mecca, which the record contradicts (29:48; 16:103). Source: Yaqeen, the Quran's engagement with Christian and Jewish literature.

E6. “Muhammad wrote it himself.” He could not read or write and dictated to scribes; the Quran's style differs sharply from his own recorded speech (hadith); it rebukes him (80:1-10; 66:1); it contains knowledge he could not have had; and it challenged his enemies to produce its like (2:23), which they never did. Verses: 2:23; 29:48; 10:16. Source: Islam Q&A 10012.

E7. The “Satanic verses” story. No authentic chain: every version is weak and none reaches the Prophet; absent from Ibn Ishaq, Bukhari and Muslim; rejected by Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi, Ibn Hazm and al-Albani. It contradicts the authentic report that the pagans prostrated at the end of Surah 53 and the surah's own anti-idol verses (53:19-23). Verses: 53:19-23; 22:52. Source: Refutation of the Satanic verses incident.

সারকথা: মূল জবাব: কুরআন মুখস্থ ও লিখিত দুইভাবেই সংরক্ষিত; এতে স্ববিরোধ নেই, এটা বাইবেলের নকল নয়, নিরক্ষর নবী নিজে লেখেননি, আর এর চ্যালেঞ্জ কেউ পূরণ করেনি। নাসখ মানে মত বদল নয়, ধাপে ধাপে বিধান; “শয়তানি আয়াত”-এর গল্পের সহিহ সনদ নেই।

Theme F: Ethics and law

F1. “Islam oppresses women.” Islam introduced consent to marriage, the dower as the woman's own property, inheritance rights and spiritual equality (4:1; 33:35) in 7th-century Arabia. The inheritance difference tracks financial obligations (men must maintain women; women keep their wealth), and in many cases women inherit equally or more. The two-witness rule is limited to specific financial contracts. Hijab (24:30-31) begins with men lowering their gaze. Polygamy (4:3) is a restricted permission, not a command. Where women are mistreated, local culture is breaking Islamic law. Sources: Yaqeen, five myths about women in Islamic law; Yaqeen, gender equity at the advent of Islam.

F2. Slavery. Islam inherited a universal institution and radically regulated it: no enslavement of free people, humane treatment, equal food and clothing, no racial basis; emancipation was made an act of worship and the expiation for many sins (90:12-13; 24:33; 4:92; 5:89), so it came close to prohibition, and Muslim jurists approved abolition (Tunis, 1846). Freed slaves became leading scholars. Sources: Yaqeen, Slavery and Islam; Mawali: how freed slaves shaped Islamic scholarship.

F3. Apostasy. The Quran prescribes no earthly penalty for changing belief and affirms “no compulsion in religion” (2:256; 10:99; 18:29; 4:137). The classical penalty rests on hadith that many scholars (Shaltut, al-Qaradawi, Alalwani) read as targeting apostasy combined with treason in wartime; the Prophet never executed anyone for private loss of faith. Present it honestly as a debate among Muslims. Source: Yaqeen, the issue of apostasy.

F4. Jihad and the “sword verse” (9:5). 9:1-7 concerns specific treaty-breaking pagans, and 9:6 commands protecting any who seek safety. Fighting is permitted only against aggression (2:190), under authority, with strict rules: never women, children, the elderly or anyone not fighting (Ibn Abbas; Abu Bakr's instructions). Islam spread in Indonesia, Malaysia and China by trade, not conquest; Muslims ruled India for centuries with a Hindu majority. Verses: 9:5-6; 2:190; 2:256; 5:32; 60:8. Source: Yaqeen, Is Islam a violent religion?.

F5. “Religion causes war.” The Encyclopedia of Wars (Phillips and Axelrod) counts about 7% of 1,763 wars as primarily religious; the 20th century's secular and atheist regimes killed far more. Misuse by adherents does not refute a religion, any more than Stalin refutes atheism. In China, make the general point and do not discuss politics. Verse: 5:32. Source: Theos, Is religion the cause of war?.

F6. The age of Aisha. Yaqeen offers several answers: by the norms of every 7th-century society she was of age, and no contemporary critic, Meccan, Jewish or Christian, objected; maturity was reached early and marriages were family-arranged across all cultures; a historical-critical minority view dates her at about 18 at consummation. Judging a 7th-century marriage by 21st-century legal ages is anachronism; Aisha became the most prolific female scholar of Islam. Sources: Understanding Aisha's age; The case for an older age.

F7. Zakir Naik's twenty common questions.
